# Student Guide — The Research Log

The Research Log is where you document every source you use during your investigation. It's not a bibliography — it's a thinking record. For each source, you don't just note what you found; you evaluate it, question it, and decide how it fits into your argument.

A well-completed Research Log is what separates research from searching. Anyone can find information. The log forces you to do something harder: think about what the information is worth and how you're going to use it.

---

## How it works

Each source gets its own entry. You fill in five fields for every source you look at — not just the ones that seem useful. An entry with a source you decided to reject is just as valuable as one you used, because it shows you were being selective rather than just collecting.

You can add as many entries as you need. Once you've finished with a source, you can collapse its entry to keep the page tidy. You can also download your entire log as a spreadsheet at any time.

---

## The five fields

### Source

Record the full reference for where this information came from. Depending on the source type, this might be:

- A website URL (include the full address, not just the homepage)
- A book title and author
- An article title and publication name
- The name of a person you interviewed or a documentary you watched
- An AI-generated response (yes, you can use AI as a source — but the reliability field becomes especially important)

Don't be vague. "A website about climate change" is useless to you when you're writing your final answer three days later. Record it properly now.

### What did you find?

Summarise the key information, facts, or arguments from this source. This is not a copy-paste — write it in your own words. What did the source actually tell you that's relevant to your investigation?

Keep it focused on what matters for your task. You don't need to summarise the entire source — just the parts that are relevant to the questions you're investigating.

### Important points

Out of everything you found, what stands out as most significant? This might be:

- A statistic or piece of data that directly supports or challenges your argument
- A definition you need for your final answer
- A quote or claim from an expert
- A finding that surprised you or that you didn't expect

This field is what you'll refer back to in the Final Answer stage. Make it genuinely useful.

### How reliable is this source?

This is where most students do the least thinking, and where the biggest marks are lost. A source being easy to find does not make it reliable. Ask yourself:

**Who wrote it?** Is there a named author? What are their credentials? Are they an expert in this field, a journalist, a campaigner, a student?

**When was it written?** Is the information current? For some topics (medicine, climate science, technology) a source from five years ago may be out of date. For others (historical events, literature) the date matters less.

**Why was it written?** Is this source trying to inform, persuade, sell, or entertain? A company publishing research about its own products, a campaign group reporting on its own cause, or a government publishing its own performance data all have interests that may shape what they include and exclude.

**Is this a primary or secondary source?** A primary source is direct evidence — a speech, a scientific study, a census, an interview. A secondary source interprets or comments on primary sources. Both are useful, but they're not the same thing.

> *Tip: A source you disagree with or find unreliable is still worth logging. Record why you don't trust it — that's critical thinking.*

### How does this support, challenge, or nuance your argument?

This is the most important field in the log. It's the question that connects your research to your thinking.

Don't just write "supports my argument." Be specific:

- **Supports:** "This source provides the unemployment statistics I need to back up my claim that the policy had a negative effect on working-class communities."
- **Challenges:** "This source argues the opposite of my position — that the policy reduced long-term unemployment. I need to address this in my answer."
- **Nuances:** "This source agrees with my main argument but adds an important condition I hadn't considered — it only applies in urban areas, not rural ones."

And watch for this trap: if every single source you log "supports my argument," you haven't been doing research — you've been confirming what you already believed. Go and find a source that pushes back.

---

## How many sources do you need?

There's no set number, but a single source is never enough for a complex question. As a rough guide:

- At least one source that directly supports your main argument
- At least one source that challenges or complicates it
- At least one source for any specific fact, statistic, or claim you make in your final answer

Quality matters more than quantity. Three well-evaluated sources are worth more than ten entries filled in with one line each.

---

## Common mistakes

**Logging only the sources that agree with you.** This is confirmation bias. Your final answer will be weaker for it, and your teacher will notice.

**Treating reliability as a formality.** "It was on a reputable website" is not a reliability evaluation. Name the author, assess their expertise, consider their potential bias.

**Filling in "important points" with copy-pasted text.** If you copy it, you probably haven't understood it. Paraphrase.

**Only logging sources after you've decided how to use them.** Log as you go — before you've committed to a position. That way your research shapes your argument, not the other way around.

---

## When to move on

You're ready to leave RESEARCH when:

- You have at least two or three solid, well-evaluated entries
- You have at least one source that challenges your position (and you've noted how you'll deal with it)
- You could explain what each source contributes to your argument without looking at your notes
- You have enough evidence to write a well-supported final answer

When you move to COMPASS, your Research Log will be waiting — you'll need to tick off that you've actually done what this guide describes.
