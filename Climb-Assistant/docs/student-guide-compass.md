# Student Guide — The COMPASS Stage

COMPASS is where you stop and take stock before you write. You've investigated in TORCH, logged your sources in RESEARCH — now you need to check that your thinking is actually ready. COMPASS is not about finding new information. It's about making sure the information you have is solid enough to build an answer on.

There are two parts: a **research integrity checklist** and **five reflection prompts**.

---

## Part 1 — Research integrity checklist

Before you think about writing, tick each of these honestly. They're not trick questions — they're a quick audit of whether your research actually meets the standard required.

**Sources recorded** — Did you log every source you looked at, not just the ones that ended up being useful? A source you decided to reject is still worth recording — it shows you considered it.

**Reliability checked** — For each source, did you think about who wrote it, when, and why? A website with no named author, a news article from ten years ago, or a blog post from someone with an obvious agenda all need flagging — not ignoring.

**Bias considered** — Every source has a perspective. A government report, a newspaper editorial, a peer-reviewed study, and a social media post all come from somewhere. Did you think about what angle each source might be coming from?

**Balance tested** — Did you actively look for sources that challenge your conclusion — not just ones that support it? If every source you found agrees with what you already thought, that's a warning sign, not a success.

**Evidence-led** — This is the most important one. Does your conclusion follow from the evidence, or did you decide what you believed first and then find sources to back it up? Be honest. Working backwards from a pre-formed opinion is one of the most common research errors and one of the hardest to spot in your own work.

Tick these only when you can genuinely say yes. If you can't tick one, that's a signal to go back to RESEARCH.

---

## Part 2 — Five reflection checks

These are written prompts, not yes/no questions. Each one asks you to think carefully about where you are before you commit to writing your final answer. There are no right answers — only honest ones.

### 1. Say it in your own words

Write your key conclusion or argument as if you're explaining it to someone who knows nothing about the topic. No notes. No copying from a source.

This is a test of genuine understanding. If you can't do it without looking at your notes, you don't understand it well enough to write about it yet — and that's useful to know now, not halfway through your final answer.

> *Tip: If you find yourself reaching for a phrase you've read somewhere, stop. Say it differently. Your own words, even if they're clunkier, show more real understanding than a polished phrase you've borrowed.*

### 2. The strongest challenge to your thinking

What is the best argument or evidence that goes against your current position? Write it down — not dismissively, but fairly.

This is not about changing your mind. It's about showing that you've genuinely engaged with the other side. A good answer doesn't pretend the counterargument doesn't exist — it acknowledges it and explains why your position still holds.

> *Tip: If you can't think of a serious challenge to your position, you probably haven't looked hard enough. Go back to RESEARCH and specifically search for the opposing view.*

### 3. Where you're least confident

What part of your understanding is shakiest? Where would you struggle most if someone pushed back?

Not knowing something is not a failure — it's information. This prompt helps you identify the exact gap you need to address before you write. Being honest here is far better than writing a confident-sounding answer that collapses under scrutiny.

> *Tip: If your answer to this is "nothing — I'm confident about everything," read it again more carefully. There's almost always something. The students who find it now write better answers than the ones who find it in the teacher's feedback.*

### 4. Is your evidence balanced?

Look across all your sources together — not one at a time. Do they represent a range of perspectives, or do they all point in the same direction? Is there a significant viewpoint that's completely absent from your research?

This is different from the checklist question above. Here you're asking not just whether you looked for balance, but whether you actually achieved it. A collection of sources that all agree isn't balanced research — it's confirmation bias with footnotes.

> *Tip: If all your sources share the same basic position, name the missing perspective here. You don't have to agree with it, but you should acknowledge it exists.*

### 5. Are you ready to write?

Looking at everything honestly — your understanding, your sources, your gaps, your confidence — are you ready to write a well-supported answer?

If yes, move on. If not, what specifically is still missing? Name it. Then go back and fix it before you move to the Final Response stage.

---

## When to move on

You're ready to leave COMPASS when:

- All five integrity checklist items are ticked
- All five reflection checks are completed with genuine, specific answers (not "yes" or "I think so")
- You can state your argument clearly in your own words
- You know what the strongest counterargument is and how you'd respond to it
- You're confident enough in your evidence to write from it, not around it

> **Remember:** COMPASS saves automatically. If you're not ready to write yet, use what you've found here to guide one more pass through RESEARCH. That's not going backwards — that's the process working as it should.
